# risk-assessment-structure

## When to invoke

Use this for any question asking for a risk assessment, directional view,
investment thesis, covenant read, capital-structure concern, disclosure gap, or
comparison between similar-looking outcomes.

## Core shape

Risk analysis should be explicit about mechanism, timing, severity, and what
would change the view. Do not stop at "material risk" or "monitor going
forward." A useful equity-research answer says why the risk matters, how it
transmits into cash flow, liquidity, dilution, covenant headroom, valuation, or
strategic flexibility, and when it could become visible.

### 1. Name the mechanism

Weak:

"Buybacks were not used in this period, which could be for various reasons."

Stronger:

"Buyback non-use in both Q2 2020 and Q2 2021 is more consistent with a binding
covenant or liquidity constraint than a discretionary capital-allocation choice.
The signal persists across different operating backdrops: pandemic liquidity
pressure in 2020 and M&A capital reservation in 2021."

### 2. Commit to a directional conclusion

Weak:

"The AQR cushion is something to monitor."

Stronger:

"Net assessment: the AQR cushion is thin. At 8-13 bps above threshold, one
quarter of stressed operating cash flow can move the company into cash dominion
before a formal covenant breach."

### 3. Rank by decision relevance

When multiple risks exist, sort them by a clear basis: velocity, reversibility,
binary vs gradual impact, disclosure dependence, liquidity impact, dilution
impact, or earnings sensitivity.

Example:

1. Change-of-control default: fastest and binary if triggered at closing.
2. AQR covenant pressure: medium velocity; depends on quarter-end working
   capital and streamline mechanics.
3. Refinancing wall: slower; material only if capital markets or lender appetite
   deteriorate before maturity.

### 4. Distinguish similar structures

Do not merge instruments or terms that behave differently.

Example:

"The bond-hedge leg and the warrant leg are asymmetric. The bond hedge offsets
conversion dilution when the notes convert. The warrants are a residual
short-volatility position whose dilution depends on exercise price, settlement
method, and share price at exercise. Treating the convertible program as one
unit hides that the incremental dilution risk lives on the warrant side."

### 5. State missing disclosures

Name the facts that would move the conclusion.

Example:

"The key missing disclosure is whether the lender waived or amended the
change-of-control provision. Without that, the filing supports identifying the
provision as a live risk, but not concluding that a default actually occurred."

### 6. Separate assumption from fact

Use assumption framing when the filing does not answer the question directly.

Example:

"This assumes the warrants settle in shares rather than cash. If cash-settled,
the risk shifts from dilution to liquidity."

## Useful patterns

### Two-mechanism argument

"X creates the exposure; Y amplifies it."

Use for covenant structures, refinancing walls, segment concentration, customer
concentration, or margin bridge work.

### Counterfactual argument

"If X were true, we would expect Y; the filing shows Z."

Use this to avoid narrative-only explanations. It forces the memo to compare an
observed fact with the expected evidence under an alternate interpretation.

### Primary vs contributing vs conditional risk

"The primary risk is X; Y is a contributing factor; Z matters only if A occurs."

Use this when several facts point in the same direction but do not carry equal
causal weight.

## Anti-patterns

- "This could impact the business in various ways."
- "Further analysis is warranted."
- "There are several factors to consider."
- "It would be prudent to monitor this."
- "On one hand X, on the other hand Y" with no conclusion.
- Restating the question as the answer.
- Listing adjectives without facts attached.

## Final check

Before writing the final memo, confirm it includes:

- A named mechanism.
- A committed directional conclusion.
- A ranked view when risks compete.
- At least one explicit assumption if the evidence is incomplete.
- The most important missing disclosure or fact that would change the answer.
